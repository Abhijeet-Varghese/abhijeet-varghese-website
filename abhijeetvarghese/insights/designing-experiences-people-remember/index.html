Designing Experiences People Remember

The goal of experience design is not to make people see more. It is to make something worth remembering.

By Abhijeet VargheseExperience Design · Storytelling · Immersive Systems

Experience Design · 12 min

Design for the memory, not just the moment.

An experience is not the screen, room or headset in front of someone. It is what happens to them as they move through it.

In this insight

A practical view of experience architecture — from attention to lasting recall.

Memory is the mediumStory is the architectureImmersion with purposeDesigning for recallThe Memory Test

People rarely remember everything an experience contains. They remember what the experience made them feel, understand, discover and tell someone else.

People do not remember everything you designed. They remember moments.

A room they walked into and immediately understood. A story that suddenly made sense. A detail they were not expecting. The feeling of being personally welcomed. The moment a physical environment changed around them.

The first time they experienced something through VR. The interaction that made a complicated idea simple. The visual that stayed in their head long after they left.

Those moments are rarely remembered because they contained the most technology. They are remembered because something happened to the person experiencing them.

Across visual communication, animation, brand systems, immersive technology, VR, interactive environments and physical-digital experiences, the same question keeps returning:

That is a much more difficult question than: What should we put into the experience?

Memory is the real medium.

Designers often think in visible outputs: screens, graphics, animation, architecture, typography, motion and interfaces. But there is another medium that matters just as much: memory.

A person may spend ten minutes inside an experience, but the experience may remain with them for years. The physical or digital artefact is temporary. The memory can be much longer.

Instead of asking what the visitor should see, ask what they should remember — why they should remember it — and what emotional or intellectual meaning that memory should carry.

01

The Experience Memory Chain

The sequence begins with attention — not technology — and is only complete when it can be recalled.

AttentionCuriosityParticipationDiscoveryEmotionMeaningMemoryRecall

A technologically impressive experience can still fail if it creates attention but never reaches meaning.

The experience is not the interface.

A screen is not an experience. A VR headset is not an experience. A projection wall, a beautifully designed room and a website are not experiences either. They are components of one.

The actual experience happens in the mind of the person moving through them. They perceive, interpret, make choices, form expectations, become curious, become confused, understand, feel something, remember — and eventually leave.

Experience design is not simply about designing what people encounter. It is about designing what happens to people as they encounter it.

Meaning before medium.

One of the biggest mistakes in experience design is starting with the medium: “Let’s create a VR experience,” “Let’s build an interactive wall,” or “Let’s use projection mapping.” These are production directions, not experience strategies.

The better starting point is: What do we want the person to understand, feel or do? Only then should we ask what medium can make that happen most effectively. Technology becomes powerful when it has a job.

02

Meaning Before Medium

A human-first hierarchy for choosing technology and physical mechanisms.

Human outcomeStoryEmotionInteractionMediumDetail

The flow reverses a technology-first brief. Start with the human outcome; choose the enabling technology later.

Chapter 02

Information becomes meaningful when it is placed in a narrative sequence.

Story is the invisible architecture.

Storytelling is not reserved for film. A building can tell a story. A product demonstration can tell a story. A museum, website, VR simulation and corporate experience centre can all tell a story.

Story is the structure through which information becomes meaningful. The physical environment is what people move through. The story is what their mind moves through.

A memorable experience has progression

“Here is information. Here is another screen. Here is another video.” is a collection. A stronger experience introduces something, creates a question, invites exploration, reveals something, makes the visitor participate and resolves with meaning.

Progression creates anticipation. Anticipation creates attention. Attention creates engagement. Engagement creates the conditions for memory.

03

The Experience Arc

Every phase answers a different human question — what do I notice, why does this matter, what will I take away?

ArrivalOrientationCuriosityParticipationDiscoveryEmotionResolutionRecall

Immersion is not enough.

Large screens, VR, 360° video, spatial audio and interactive environments can all be immersive. But immersion itself is not the objective.

A person can feel completely surrounded by an environment and still leave without understanding anything. VR can create presence very effectively; the creative challenge is everything that comes after.

04

The Immersion Stack

The deeper an experience moves through the stack, the greater its potential to become meaningful.

PresenceInteractionDiscoveryMeaningEmotionMemory

The body is part of the interface

The body remembers differently.

Physical experiences involve movement, sound, scale, distance, material, light, rhythm and human proximity. You do not simply see the environment. You occupy it.

The act of entering, moving through and interacting with a space becomes part of the story itself.

Chapter 03

Space, interaction and narrative must work as one connected journey.

The environment can become part of the narrative.

Lighting can change as a visitor reaches a certain moment. Sound can shift as the story progresses. A wall can respond to presence. A visual can appear only after a decision. A physical transition can separate one chapter from the next.

The environment is not merely displaying the story. The environment is telling the story. This is where spatial computing, interactive media, sensors, projection, VR and environmental technology become genuinely interesting — when they serve the narrative.

One journey, not a technology showroom.

The Orange Business New Executive Briefing Center in Mumbai showed why experiences cannot be designed as disconnected technology installations. It brought together physical space, brand identity, digital content, video, interactive media, VR, registration, personalised communication and environmental automation.

The creative challenge was not to make each component impressive independently. It was to make the journey feel coherent: natural from arrival to understanding, rather than a collection of demonstrations. The technology had different jobs. The story had one direction.

Personalisation can create memory.

Recognition is powerful. A name appearing in the right context, content responding to presence, an environment adapting to a journey or an interaction reflecting what someone just did can turn an anonymous interaction into a personal one.

But personalisation should make a person feel: “This experience is for me.” Not: “The system knows information about me.” Good personalisation feels natural; bad personalisation feels intrusive.

The smallest moment can become the strongest memory.

Memorable experiences do not always depend on giant moments. A precisely timed door, a sound cue, a pause before a reveal, an unexpected object or one simple interaction that makes someone smile can be enough.

These details create contrast. If everything is spectacular, nothing is spectacular. If every screen is interactive, interaction becomes ordinary. Memorable design needs rhythm.

05

The Rhythm of Experience

Contrast creates attention. The pause is as important as the reveal.

ExpectationTensionDiscoveryPauseRewardReflectionNext expectation

Continuous stimulation removes contrast. Rhythm gives attention somewhere to land.

Cognitive load can destroy memory.

Too many screens, too much text, too many choices, simultaneous animations and competing interactions leave little room to make meaning. When everything asks for attention, nothing receives enough.

A person should understand where to look, what matters, what to do and what happens next. Clarity creates cognitive space. Cognitive space creates room for meaning. Meaning is what memory needs.

Chapter 04

A strong experience continues through reflection, conversation and retelling.

The experience does not end at exit.

An experience begins before the first screen appears: in the invitation, expectation, arrival and first visual. It continues through the main story and final moment — and into the conversation afterwards.

A genuinely memorable experience often survives because people explain it, photograph it, show it to someone else or describe how it felt. The experience becomes a story. The story becomes social memory.

Design the full arc: expectation before, meaning during and reflection after.

Reflection

Designing for recall.

One of the most useful questions is: If someone had only one sentence to describe this experience tomorrow, what would I want that sentence to be?

“It is a room with lots of screens” suggests an identity problem. “I stepped inside and suddenly understood what the company was trying to change” leaves a story.

Experience Compression

Behind-the-scenes complexity should resolve into one meaningful takeaway.

The design challenge is not to eliminate complexity. It is to stop unnecessary complexity becoming the visitor’s burden.

Technology should disappear.

When the experience works, the interaction feels natural, the story feels obvious and the person stays focused on the outcome rather than the mechanism.

Chapter 05

The objective is not maximum intensity. It is the right response for the context.

Not every experience needs to be unforgettable.

Some experiences need to be fast, clear, efficient, quiet and predictable. A banking transaction does not need to become a theatrical performance. A navigation system does not need a narrative arc.

Experience design is not about adding emotion everywhere. It is about creating the right human response for the situation.

08

Experience Intensity Spectrum

Choose the appropriate level of intensity — transformation is not always the goal.

EfficiencyClarityEngagementEmotionImmersionTransformation

What makes something memorable?

There is no single formula. But relevance, contrast, participation, meaning, emotion and story repeatedly make memory more likely. Experience design is choreography: deciding where someone stands, looks, listens, pauses, moves, discovers, expects, understands and remembers.

The audience is not watching the design. They are moving through it.

The future is not more immersion.

Higher resolution, larger displays, more sensors, better tracking, more realistic environments and more intelligent AI can all be valuable. But capability is not the same as meaning.

The future should be more intentional, relevant, adaptive, human and memorable. Move from “Can we make it interactive?” to “What should respond?” From “Can AI generate it?” to “What should AI help a person discover?” Better questions produce better experiences.

Framework 09

The Memory Test.

At the end of a design process, reduce the entire experience to five clear outcomes. Select each prompt to explore what it tests.

01

One image: What will they remember seeing? A singular visual anchor gives an experience a durable mental shape.

02

One feeling: What will they remember feeling? Emotion gives information weight and makes it personally relevant.

03

One idea: What will they remember understanding? A clear idea turns stimulation into a meaningful takeaway.

04

One action: What will they do afterwards? A valuable experience makes a next step feel relevant rather than imposed.

05

One story: What will they tell someone else? If the experience can travel as a story, it can continue beyond the room.

Final thought

A memorable experience needs more intention.

Not simply what people will see or photograph — what they will carry with them when they leave.
